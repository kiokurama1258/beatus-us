How Much Does a New Dental Patient Cost? How to Calculate It

By Bruno Vasconcellos, founder of Beatus Mídias
October 1, 2026 · 6 min read
Direct answer: The cost per new dental patient is your total marketing cost for a period divided by the number of new patients who actually came in during that period. Compare it with what a new patient is worth over time, based on your own records, and you know how much you can invest. Cost per click and cost per lead only explain where that final number comes from.

Which formulas do you need?

Dental marketing metrics and how to calculate them
MetricHow to calculate
Cost per leadAd spend divided by calls, forms and messages that are real inquiries
Booking rateLeads that booked divided by total leads
Show ratePatients who came in divided by patients who booked
Cost per new patientTotal marketing cost divided by new patients who came in
New patient valueAverage revenue per new patient over a period you choose, such as the first year, from your own records

How does it work in practice?

The example below uses hypothetical numbers, only to show the math.

Illustrative example with hypothetical numbers
StepNumberResult
Ad spend$3,000
Real inquiries100$30 per lead
Booked4040% booking rate
Showed up30$100 per new patient

Now improve only the follow-up. If 50 of those 100 leads book and 40 show up, the same $3,000 brings 40 patients at $75 each. Nothing changed in the ads. That is why follow-up is part of marketing, not an afterthought.

What is a new patient worth?

Use your own records: what new patients spend in their first year, how many stay for hygiene and how many accept larger treatment. Include management fees and software on the cost side. With both numbers in hand, you can decide how much you can pay per patient and still grow profitably.

Which mistakes distort the number?

  • Counting clicks or page views as leads.
  • Ignoring phone calls, a major source of dental inquiries.
  • Leaving management fees and software out of the cost.
  • Judging a campaign in its first weeks, before it has enough data.
  • Comparing your cost with numbers from other markets instead of your own patient value.

The bottom line

Know your cost per new patient and your new patient value, and marketing stops being an expense you hope works. It becomes a number you can manage, improve and scale.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cost per new patient for a dental practice?

It depends on your market, the treatments you promote and what a new patient is worth to your practice. A cost that works for an implant practice may be too high for a practice focused on hygiene. Instead of chasing a benchmark from another market, compare your cost per new patient with your own patient value.

Should the management fee be included in the cost per patient?

Yes. The true cost of acquiring a patient includes ad spend, agency or staff time, software and any other marketing expense. Leaving those out makes campaigns look cheaper than they are and leads to bad decisions about where to invest. Track ad cost separately if you like, but decide on the full cost.

How long until the cost per new patient stabilizes?

Usually a few weeks of data, and longer for treatments with a long decision cycle such as implants. The early weeks include learning time for both the campaigns and the team. Look at trends over a month or more, and judge each treatment separately instead of reacting to a single week.
